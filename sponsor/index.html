Tap each question to open it

1Can you bring me new customers?

Who could you introduce me to, and what are they likely to need? If you know five drivers looking for Rotax parts or help getting a kart race-ready, tell me. If we run an offer together, I'd want to track the enquiries and sales it brings in.

2Can you give my customers a good day out?

Could you host some of my customers at a race, introduce them to your team and show them around the paddock? Or could you use your connections to organise a karting afternoon where they race each other? Tell me where, how many people you can host and what it would cost.

3Can you get me seen beyond your own channels?

Could you get my logo on a championship livestream or in the official programme, or get us a mention in the organiser's YouTube highlights? I'd want to know what the organiser has confirmed, where the brand will appear and who's likely to see it.

4What would my backing help you achieve?

If you're a young driver from our area, I'd like to know what my support would make possible. Is it your first club season, or a step up to national racing? Could you also bring your kart or race car to my events across the year, such as showroom open days, so my customers can meet you and see what I'm backing?

5Can you give me photos or videos I can use?

Could you send me photos or a short video showing one of our products in use on a race weekend? Tell me what you'd make, when I'd get it, and whether I could use it on my website, in emails or on social media.

6Can you introduce me to a business I could work with?

Say you introduce me to a major supplier at the track and that relationship turns into profitable business. I might agree to put 10% of the profit from that introduction towards your racing: £5,000 of agreed profit would mean £500 in sponsorship. We'd agree upfront how to calculate the profit and how long it runs.

    Mistakes that lose sponsors
    
    
    Tap one to see the fix
    
    
    Relying on email
    Almost every cold sponsorship email is ignored. Go in person, ask for the person who makes marketing decisions, and book a meeting with them. Bring the proposal to that meeting.
    
    
    Talking to the wrong person
    Reception and junior staff can't say yes. Find out who the owner, managing director or marketing manager is (their website or LinkedIn will help) and get in front of them.
    
    
    Sending the same email to everyone
    "Dear Sir/Madam" goes straight in the bin. Use their name, mention their business, and say why you picked them.
    
    
    Only asking, never offering
    A sponsor is buying something. List exactly what they get: logo places, posts, videos, guest days and reports.
    
    
    No numbers
    Give followers, views, how many people see you race and how many meetings you do. Small honest numbers beat vague big claims.
    
    
    No clear ask
    Say how much, or which product, and what it pays for. "Any support would be great" is easy to ignore.
    
    
    Writing an essay
    Keep the email under 200 words and the proposal to one page. Put the details in the attachment.
    
    
    Bad photos, or none
    Send two or three sharp, well-lit photos of you and the kart. Mock up their logo on the kart if you can.
    
    
    Going quiet after the deal
    Send a report after every round, tag them every time, and say thank you. That's how a sponsor renews next year.
    
    
    Giving up after one email
    Follow up politely after a week, then once more. Many sponsors say yes on the second or third message.
    
    
    Starting too big
    Local businesses say yes far more often than big brands. Start local, build a track record, then go bigger.
